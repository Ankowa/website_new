What doesn't kill you may teach you something. I believe this is the most truthful variation of the famous saying, which in original phrasing is absurdly stupid. It's unreasonable to expect that traumatic or tragic experiences will make one stronger. Oftentimes it's the opposite, but I digress. While the saying is broadly understood to mean **bad** stuff that happen to a person can build some form of strength or resilience, I interpret it more broadly. Being alive is somewhat akin to *experiencing*, as each day is a sequence of experiences, or events, as [I had already written before](https://antonikowalczuk.com/blog/chase/#the-today-is-a-story). Since all of your experiences but the final one **do not kill you**, let's rephrase the saying as "what you experience may teach you something". Sounds trivial, but hold on (or how do you say that?), there is depth that I hope to explore. 

Something about prior, and then connect it to the experience, and what kind of experiences there are wrt to the prior: not novel, novel, revolutionary, random noise. What is prior btw? Need to define that for sure. Experience is reality imposing itself on the prior. Like prior is an workable approximation on how the world is, how people are, and how I (the person with the prior) is. It's the set of rules, prejudices, biases, filters, interpretation mechanisms, acquired implicitly (at least most of them). Since it's an approximation, it's imperfect. It can't be perfect (duh), so inevitably some experiences (i.e., instances of reality) will be in conflict with this approximation. Conflict being: my prior predicted X, and Y happened, which is totally in opposite to X, or being: my prior predicted 10, and reality gave me 10 + delta. So basically: each approximation has errors, and some errors are bigger than the other, and some errors are within expected error margin, and some are fucking off-the-charts.

And the question now is: what should happen to the prior once a new experience with high prior error has happened? I would suggest that the prior should be *calibrated* to better match the reality it interacts with. This is also called learning. You thought X will happen, and Y happened. So you made an error, and that's ok. What's even better is that the next time you'd predict Y, and Y will happen. But to get there, the prior needs to be updated. Calibration is not only about errors. It can also be about entirely new perspectives which have been opened up due to experiencing something extraordinary. Kinda like an enrichment of prior, which can add depth to it, and not necessarily *calibrate* the existing bias to better match reality. This is also a very nice idea. Yes, also getting an answer to a very important question will calibrate the prior, but not in a "changing parameters" meaning of the word "calibration".

So while the experience with high prior error (that's a nice name, introduce it somewhere) will likely not kill you, it often really should teach you something. (this is a nice conclusion, idk where to put it tho, maybe define what teaching is?)

Okay, maybe once we know what prior is, what is an experience, what is learning, time to introduce a class of experiences: *calibrating events*? And maybe think about how one should go after such events, or how to react to these, how to distinguish noise from actual signal. Basically this also goes to something related to challenging oneself. Actively seeking calibration is throwing your prior at a problem which the current prior cannot fully solve, and while you try to solve it, you learn something new (INSIGHT!), and update your prior with new knowledge. This also can be connected to fucking flow, which is basically acquiring insight with a feedback loop and a challenge that is not too easy but not to hard, challenging enough, and matters.
